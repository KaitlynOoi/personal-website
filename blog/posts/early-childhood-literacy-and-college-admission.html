Early Childhood Literacy Exposure and Selective College Admission

Kaitlyn Ooi Professor Alison Minami

When I volunteered with one of 826LA’s programs that supports underserved students with college applications, I met a student named Jade who comes from a non-English speaking, low-income immigrant household. As the oldest kid, she was expected to be the caretaker of her younger siblings when her parents were at work. Hence, Jade spent most of her childhood managing household responsibilities. She excelled in school and extracurricular activities despite these barriers, earning the GPA needed for admission to her dream school, UC Berkeley. However, while her grades reflect strong academic performance, they do not represent her storytelling skills. Jade never had the chance to explore English literacy when she was younger. Her first proper English class was when she took AP literature in her junior year. Now, Jade fears that her essays won’t stand out among competitive applicants because she is not confident in using her storytelling skills to shape her experiences into compelling narratives. Her story reveals a gap in the conversation of college admission: Students from low-SES (socioeconomic status) families, especially students from non-native English-speaking households, who lack storytelling skills, are less likely to be admitted to selective colleges despite having the same academic potential.

Jade’s concern reflects a larger shift in the criteria for recent college admissions. As colleges have adopted test-optional policies, admissions officers have begun to evaluate students more holistically, considering their high school GPA, extracurricular involvement, letters of recommendation, and application essays. According to David Hawkins, chief education and policy officer at the National Association for College Admission Counseling, selective colleges are placing more emphasis on essays than ever before (qtd. in Whitford). Hence, given two applicants with similar academic profiles and competencies, application essays often become the deciding factor in selective college admissions.

However, admission officers often lack the time to evaluate each essay in detail in the context of the writer’s background. Educational researchers Bastedo and Bowman stated that admissions officers only have around 15 minutes to read and score each file (68). When forced to make quick judgments, they may unintentionally marginalize essays that do not align with conventional expectations of academic English. Most importantly, Bastedo and Bowman found that when admissions officers were actually given detailed information about a student’s school and family context, they became more likely to admit the low-SES applicant (72). In reality, admission officers often have to review a large pool of applicants and are not capable of conducting a background search on every applicant. As a result, the fast-paced admissions process can disadvantage students from low-SES backgrounds whose difficulties are not fully considered and whose potential is not fully seen through their application essays.

Selective colleges often measure the characteristics and potential of a student based on their storytelling skills, which is a skill developed from an early age. Children who grow up surrounded by books and regular reading experiences naturally develop narrative skills before they enter a classroom. To understand how storytelling ability is formed, we must refer to the research conducted by Oriana Incognito and Giuliana Pinto, experts in the field of school psychology. In their research, a high education level in parents and a good home literacy environment are associated with high SES and vice versa (9822). Data shows that children from highly educated families score higher in literacy assessment at the start of preschool (9824). After a year of schooling, they found that emergent literacy skills, such as phonological and notational skills, in kids are improved, while deeper skills, such as textual skills, remain predominantly related to family experiences (9825). The research defined phonological and notational skills as the ability to detect rhymes and decode letters, which is the foundation for reading and writing, while textual competency refers to narrative skills, which are the basis for storytelling (9822). These research results tell us that a structured syllabus can improve children’s reading and writing skills because grammar and alphabetical structure can be taught and learn easily. But when it comes to abstract ideas such as storytelling, it is difficult for teachers to instill them in students. This indicates that literacy gaps children inherit from their parents and home environments do not simply disappear when they enter school.

While early literacy exposure level already places low-SES students at a disadvantage, these challenges intensify for students from immigrant households where English is not the primary language. In fact, developing storytelling skills in English requires more than just vocabulary. It relies on years of cultural exposure to the usage of English. Researchers at the Migration Policy Institute have shown that one-third of immigrants in the U.S. live in low-income households (Gelatt et al. 1). Additionally, 47% of immigrant adults have limited English proficiency (Rao et al. 1), which reduces the likelihood of children receiving language-rich conversations or regular reading activities at home. This reality is reflected in Jade’s experience. Growing up in a Spanish-speaking household and attending a school with a predominantly Hispanic student population, she had fewer opportunities to be exposed to English language narrative practices. Yet when she applies to college, she is still held to the same expectations as native English speakers from high-SES families. Hence, it is unfair to expect non-native English-speaking students from low-income households to be assessed as if they had equal access to literacy development and storytelling opportunities.

Although low-SES students, especially immigrant students, face structural barriers to developing storytelling skills, it doesn’t necessarily mean that all students from privileged backgrounds excel at writing. Thus, some may argue that SES is not always linked to narrative ability. I agree with this statement to a certain extent. Doubtlessly, students from high SES families might have weak storytelling skills. However, they often have the financial stability to support them in getting external guidance for essays. To elaborate, when personal statements become a major factor in admissions, rich families can simply purchase support. The Harvard Crimson reports that private college-consulting firms now provide services such as personal statement editing, application strategy, and extracurricular packaging specifically tailored for applicants to selective universities, which costs up to $10,000 (“The Business of Getting In”). This amount is almost one-third of the total annual income of a family at the federal poverty level in the United States (United States Government). Low SES families simply cannot afford to pay for a private essay coach that helps write their children’s essays when they are struggling financially. In other words, privilege can compensate for gaps in literacy through professional coaching that refines or even rewrites applicants’ stories to match the expectations of selective colleges. In short, students from high SES families are never at a disadvantage.

In a world marked by equality among all, there is a duty we have to close this gap. In order to eliminate disparities, we must acknowledge the disadvantages faced by low-SES students. After interviewing Shani Foster, the Director of Education at 826LA, I realised that education advocates are always deeply aware of the gap in our conversation. To elaborate, 826LA took initiatives in the outreach of kids aged 6 to 18 by collaborating with schools. For instance, 826LA prioritizes partnerships with schools with historically marginalized populations to provide one-on-one writing help in the classroom (Foster). While recognizing the importance of literacy education in children, Ms. Foster acknowledged that the limitation of 826LA is that their outreach is mostly based in schools and doesn’t necessarily extend to home literacy engagement.

This insight leads to my next point. I discovered the potential for collaboration between 826LA and Reach Out and Read (ROR). ROR is a nonprofit that embeds literacy promotion into pediatric care from birth to age five. The program equips parents to become active partners in literacy through three main steps: one, anticipatory guidance, in which pediatricians explain the importance of reading aloud and how to make it interactive; two, book distribution, ensuring that every child receives culturally relevant books that help build a home library; and three, modeling, where clinicians demonstrate shared reading techniques during the visit (Uthirasamy et al.). Most importantly, ROR provides bilingual books and encourages caregivers to read in their home language, allowing English learners to build narrative understanding while they are still developing English proficiency. The overarching goal is to encourage low SES families to make daily reading a meaningful routine, improving children’s social-emotional development and school readiness (Uthirasamy et al. 520–21). Partnership between 826LA and ROR will provide strong literacy support to children of all ages from low SES families. The outcome is far greater than just preparing students to write strong college application essays. It will empower young people from marginalized groups to express their identities and to be seen for who they are. When children grow up with confidence in their narrative skills, they gain self-advocacy skills that expand their future opportunities far beyond the college application process.

Ultimately, the point of getting into a selective college is not merely prestige. It is the chance to change the socioeconomic status of one’s family. Although some may argue that students could simply apply to less selective schools that don’t require essays, this perspective overlooks how institutional selectivity shapes long-term economic mobility. Behavioral Science Researcher Matt S. Giani argues that selective colleges provide students with “access to academic capital rewarded in the labor market,” while less selective colleges leave students “relying more heavily on the capital acquired through their family,” which places low-SES students at a distinct disadvantage compared to their more affluent peers (456–457). At non-selective institutions, students from richer families continue to secure better jobs using their inherited connections. On the other hand, graduates from less selective colleges often fall back to the same SES they came from. This means selective colleges are often the only spaces where low-income and immigrant-background students can compete on equal footing because they can finally utilise institutional resources to achieve success. Hence, we should always encourage and provide support to children from low SES families to apply to selective colleges.

College essays were meant to allow students to express their authentic identities and to show their potential. But when students lack the storytelling skills to showcase their true selves, college essays fail their intended purpose. This is especially detrimental for students from low SES backgrounds who see selective schools as the chance to change their SES status. If we fail to address the gap in the conversation of college admissions, low SES students who have the same academic competency as their high SES peers will continue to be shut out from selective colleges. In the bigger picture, these educational barriers will sustain the cycles of poverty in low SES families. By investing in early literacy programs and partnerships, we can ensure that kids are given the same opportunity to develop storytelling skills regarding their SES background. Educators, policymakers, nonprofits, and parents are all stakeholders in this matter, and it is of utmost importance for all stakeholders to work together to address inequalities and shape a nation where every kid gets a chance to succeed.
